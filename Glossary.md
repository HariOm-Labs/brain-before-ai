# Glossary

Every technical term used in this guide, in plain English, alphabetically.

If you're reading a chapter and hit a term you don't recognize, come here first.

---

## A

**Acetylcholine (ACh)** — A modulator chemical that sharpens attention and helps the brain encode new information. Low acetylcholine → poor focus, poor learning.

**Action potential** — The electrical "spike" a neuron fires when it decides to send a signal. Lasts about 1–2 milliseconds. Same size every time — it's an all-or-nothing event.

**Active zone** — The specific spot on the presynaptic terminal where vesicles dock and release neurotransmitter. Each terminal has 5–10 of these.

**AMPA receptor** — The fast-acting "main door" receptor for glutamate. When glutamate binds, AMPA receptors open briefly and let positive charge into the receiving neuron. **The number of AMPA receptors at a synapse is what changes during learning** — a weak synapse has ~50, a strong one has ~500.

**Attenuation** — The weakening of an electrical signal as it travels down a dendrite. A +15 mV spike at the spine might only be +1 mV by the time it reaches the cell body.

**Axon** — The long output wire of a neuron. Carries signals from the cell body to distant targets. Can be microscopic or over a meter long.

**Axon hillock** — The junction where the cell body meets the axon. Has the lowest firing threshold in the neuron, so action potentials always start here.

## B

**BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor)** — A protein that supports neuron growth and synaptic plasticity. Higher BDNF → better learning. Exercise increases BDNF.

## C

**Ca²⁺ (Calcium ion)** — The most important signaling ion inside a neuron. When it enters, it triggers neurotransmitter release (at axon terminals) or the learning cascade (at dendritic spines).

**CaMKII (Calcium/Calmodulin-dependent Kinase II)** — The enzyme activated by calcium that kick-starts the learning cascade. It tags existing receptors for strengthening and recruits new ones.

**Cell body (soma)** — The main part of the neuron containing the nucleus. Where all the incoming signals get added up to decide whether to fire.

**Chloride (Cl⁻)** — A negatively charged ion used for *inhibition*. When GABA opens chloride channels, Cl⁻ flows in and makes the neuron less likely to fire.

**Consolidation** — The slow process by which temporary memories become permanent. Requires protein synthesis and mostly happens during sleep over hours to weeks.

**Coincidence detector** — A device (like the NMDA receptor) that only activates when two conditions are met simultaneously. This is how the brain notices which neurons fired *together*.

**Cortex** — The outer, wrinkled layer of the brain. Where most "higher" thinking happens.

**Cortisol** — The stress hormone. In small doses it aids memory encoding; chronically high levels impair learning and shrink synapses.

**CREB** — A transcription factor that, when activated, turns on genes involved in long-term memory formation.

## D

**Declarative memory** — Memory for facts and events you can consciously recall and put into words. ("The capital of France is Paris.")

**Dendrite** — One of the many input branches of a neuron. Receives signals from other neurons via synapses.

**Dendritic spine** — A tiny mushroom-shaped bump on a dendrite that serves as the **receiving side** of an excitatory synapse. The axon terminal of another neuron meets the spine across a tiny gap (the synaptic cleft). Spines grow when you learn and shrink when you forget. Spines never connect directly to other spines — they're always on the postsynaptic (receiving) side.

**Depolarization** — When a neuron's charge becomes less negative (closer to zero or positive). The process of becoming excited.

**Dopamine** — A modulator chemical associated with motivation, reward, and "this is important." High dopamine tags current experiences as worth remembering.

## E

**EPSP (Excitatory Postsynaptic Potential)** — The small positive voltage bump created when a neuron receives a "yes" signal from another neuron. Needs many EPSPs summing together to trigger an action potential.

## G

**GABA** — The brain's main inhibitory neurotransmitter. When GABA is released, it quiets down the receiving neuron. Opposite function of glutamate.

**Gene expression** — The process of turning on specific genes to manufacture specific proteins. Long-term memory requires it.

**Glutamate** — The brain's main excitatory neurotransmitter. The "information carrier" at 80–85% of synapses.

## H

**Hebb's Law** — "Neurons that fire together wire together." Proposed by Donald Hebb in 1949; physically implemented by NMDA receptors.

**Hippocampus** — A seahorse-shaped structure deep in the brain responsible for encoding new memories. Damage here causes severe amnesia.

**Hyperpolarization** — When a neuron's charge becomes *more* negative than resting. Happens right after an action potential and during inhibition.

## I

**Inference (in AI)** — Running an already-trained AI model on new input. **No learning happens during inference.** Weights are frozen.

**Inhibition** — Making a neuron less likely to fire. Carried out mainly by GABA.

**Ion** — An atom or molecule with a charge. The four ions that matter most for neurons: Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺.

**Ion channel** — A protein spanning the neuron's membrane that lets specific ions through when it opens. Different channels open under different conditions (voltage, neurotransmitter binding).

**IPSP (Inhibitory Postsynaptic Potential)** — The small negative voltage dip created when a neuron receives a "no" signal. Makes firing less likely.

## K

**K⁺ (Potassium)** — A positively charged ion concentrated *inside* neurons. When K⁺ channels open, K⁺ flows out, ending action potentials.

## L

**LGN (Lateral Geniculate Nucleus)** — The thalamic relay station for visual information. Signals from the retina pass through here on the way to the visual cortex.

**LTP (Long-Term Potentiation)** — The lasting strengthening of a synapse after repeated activation. The cellular basis of learning. LTD (long-term depression) is the opposite — weakening.

## M

**Metabotropic receptor** — A receptor that doesn't directly open an ion channel but instead triggers a chain of internal chemical events. Slower but longer-lasting than ionotropic receptors.

**Mg²⁺ (Magnesium)** — The ion that physically blocks NMDA receptors at rest. Pops out when the neuron depolarizes. This is why NMDA is a coincidence detector.

**Mitochondria** — Organelles inside cells that produce ATP, the energy currency of biology. Concentrated wherever neurons need lots of energy.

**Modulator** — A chemical messenger that adjusts *how* information gets processed, rather than carrying the information itself. Main ones: dopamine, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, serotonin.

**Myelin** — A fatty insulation wrapped around axons to speed up signal propagation. Disease of myelin = multiple sclerosis.

## N

**Na⁺ (Sodium)** — A positively charged ion concentrated *outside* neurons. When Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, starting action potentials.

**Neuron** — A single brain cell that receives, integrates, and sends signals. You have about 86 billion of them.

**Neurotransmitter** — The chemical released at a synapse that carries the signal to the next neuron. Glutamate, GABA, dopamine, etc.

**NMDA receptor** — The "coincidence detector" glutamate receptor. Opens only when both glutamate is bound AND the receiving neuron is already active. Triggers learning by letting calcium in.

**Node of Ranvier** — A small gap between myelin segments on an axon. The spike regenerates here, allowing fast "jumping" propagation.

**Norepinephrine (NE)** — A modulator associated with arousal and alertness. Low NE = drowsy; high NE = focused.

**Nucleus** — The DNA-containing center of a cell. In neurons, it does slow structural work (gene expression, protein synthesis), not real-time computation.

## P

**Phosphorylation** — Attaching a phosphate group to a protein, often changing its function. CaMKII phosphorylates AMPA receptors to strengthen them.

**Plasticity** — The brain's ability to change its physical structure in response to experience. Synonyms: neuroplasticity, synaptic plasticity.

**Postsynaptic** — The *receiving* side of a synapse.

**Prefrontal cortex** — The front part of the brain. Handles planning, decision-making, integration, and executive control.

**Presynaptic** — The *sending* side of a synapse.

**Procedural memory** — Memory for skills and how-to knowledge. ("How to ride a bike.") Different from declarative memory.

**Propagation** — The traveling of a signal down an axon.

**Protein synthesis** — The manufacture of new proteins from DNA instructions. Essential for long-term memory. Takes hours.

**Pruning** — The brain's process of eliminating unused synapses and neurons. Peaks in adolescence but continues throughout life.

## R

**Receptor** — A protein on the cell membrane that binds a specific chemical (like glutamate) and responds to it.

**Refractory period** — A brief time after firing when a neuron can't fire again. Limits maximum firing rate and enforces one-way signal propagation.

**REM sleep** — Rapid Eye Movement sleep, when most vivid dreams occur. Important for procedural memory and emotional processing.

**Repolarization** — The return of a neuron's charge to negative after it has spiked.

**Resting potential** — The neuron's baseline voltage when not firing. About –70 mV.

## S

**Saltatory conduction** — The "jumping" propagation of action potentials between Nodes of Ranvier in a myelinated axon. Much faster than continuous propagation.

**Serotonin** — A modulator associated with mood, patience, and persistence. Low serotonin → give up faster; depression is associated with serotonin dysfunction.

**Slow-wave sleep** — Deep, early-night sleep. Critical for consolidating declarative memories.

**SNARE complex** — The molecular machine that fuses synaptic vesicles with the terminal membrane to release neurotransmitter.

**Soma** — Another name for the cell body.

**Spaced repetition** — Studying material at increasing intervals. Works better than cramming because each re-exposure strengthens synapses that have partially decayed.

**Spike** — Another name for action potential.

**Synapse** — The connection point between two neurons. Includes the presynaptic terminal, the tiny gap (cleft), and the postsynaptic receptors.

**Synaptic cleft** — The tiny gap (20–40 nanometers) between two neurons at a synapse, across which neurotransmitter diffuses.

**Synaptotagmin** — The calcium-sensing protein on synaptic vesicles that triggers neurotransmitter release.

## T

**Terminal (axon terminal)** — The end of an axon branch where neurotransmitter gets released. One neuron can have thousands of them.

**Thalamus** — A brain structure that relays sensory information to the cortex. Controlled by attention signals.

**Threshold** — The voltage at which a neuron commits to firing an action potential. Usually around –55 mV.

**Training (in AI)** — The phase when an AI model adjusts its weights based on examples. Distinct from inference, when weights are frozen.

**Transcription factor** — A protein (like CREB) that controls which genes get turned on. Key to long-term memory.

## V

**Vesicle** — A small bubble inside an axon terminal containing thousands of neurotransmitter molecules. Fuses with the terminal membrane to release its contents.

**Voltage-gated channel** — An ion channel that opens or closes based on the voltage of the membrane. Sodium, potassium, and calcium all have voltage-gated channels.

## W

**Weight (in AI)** — The strength of a connection between two artificial neurons. Rough analog of a synapse. Adjusted during training.

**White matter** — Brain tissue composed mainly of myelinated axons. Looks white because of the fatty myelin. Forms the "highways" connecting different brain regions.

**Working memory** — The mental workspace where you hold and manipulate information actively. Capacity is small: about 3–9 items. Located primarily in the prefrontal cortex.
