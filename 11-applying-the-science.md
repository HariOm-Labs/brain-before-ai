## 1. Active recall (not re-reading)

**What to do:** After reading or listening to material, close the book and try to reproduce the key ideas from memory. Write them down. Say them out loud. Summarize without looking.

**Why it works:** Re-reading *feels* productive. It mostly isn't. It re-activates already-active synapses without strengthening them much. **Active recall forces the brain to retrieve a memory trace from storage**, which triggers LTP at exactly the synapses involved. You're not just reviewing — you're building.

**Rule of thumb:** Spend at least half your study time retrieving, not re-reading.

**Common forms:** Flashcards. Practice problems. Self-testing. The "blank page" test (write everything you remember about a topic on a blank sheet, then check what you missed).

---

## 2. Spaced repetition

**What to do:** Review material at expanding intervals — 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month.

**Why it works:** Each time synapses have *partially decayed* and then get reactivated, the rebuild is stronger than the original. Immediate re-review gives diminishing returns. Spaced review compounds. (See [Chapter 10](10-sleep-memory-forgetting.md) for the forgetting-curve math.)

**How to actually do it:**
- Use software like Anki — it automates the scheduling.
- Or keep a simple log: mark when you first learned something and schedule reviews on a calendar.
- Even crude spacing (yesterday's notes today, last week's notes this week) beats cramming.

**Counterintuitive consequence:** The material you *feel less confident about* is actually the material you should review more. Confidence without testing is misleading — your synapses might be quietly weakening and you wouldn't know until you tried to retrieve.

---

## 3. Interleaving (mixing topics)

**What to do:** Mix different topics or problem types within a single study session, instead of doing big blocks of one topic.

**Why it works:** Blocked practice creates fluency at recognizing "this is a force problem" within a known context. **Interleaving forces the brain to first identify what kind of problem it is before solving it** — which builds the deeper recognition networks that work in novel situations.

**Example:** Instead of "30 force problems, then 30 energy problems, then 30 momentum problems," do 10 of each type mixed randomly. Performance during practice feels *worse*. Performance on the actual test is *better*.

**Warning:** Interleaving feels harder than blocked practice. Students often reject it because it feels unproductive. **It feels harder because it's doing more work** — exactly the work that matters.

---

## 4. Elaboration (the "why" question)

**What to do:** After learning a new fact or concept, ask *why* it's true and *how* it connects to what you already know. Force yourself to explain it in multiple ways.

**Why it works:** Each elaboration activates a different brain region and creates new cross-links between the new concept and existing networks. Depth of processing is the strongest predictor of retention. (Recall the deep-vs-shallow learner from [Chapter 4](04-learning-mechanisms.md) — same material, completely different outcome based on number of processing angles.)

**Concrete techniques:**

- **Feynman technique** — explain the concept as if to a 12-year-old. Where you get stuck is where your understanding is weak. Find that weak spot and study it.
- **Dual coding** — pair verbal explanations with visual diagrams. Draw what you're learning.
- **Self-generated examples** — for every abstract concept, create your own concrete example from your life. Don't reuse the textbook's examples.
- **"How would I know this is wrong?"** — forces you to engage with the concept critically rather than memorize it passively.

---

## 5. Connect to prior knowledge — deliberately

**What to do:** When learning anything new, explicitly ask: *what does this remind me of? what have I already learned that's similar? how is it different?*

**Why it works:** New synapses need existing networks to cross-link into. **A concept connected to 20 things you already know is held in place by 20 threads. A concept floating on its own hangs by one.**

This is also why experts in a field learn new information in that field disproportionately fast — they already have the scaffolding. The "rich get richer" effect in learning is real and biological.

**If you're new to a field:** Spend disproportionate early time on the *foundational* concepts — even when it feels slow and unrewarding. The scaffolding you build early pays compound interest for everything that comes later. Skipping foundations to get to the "interesting" parts faster always backfires within a few months.

---

## 6. Sleep strategically

**What to do:** Study right before sleep. Get 7–9 hours. Protect the last third of your night (REM time). Take short naps after important study sessions if possible.

**Why it works:** You now know. Slow-wave sleep consolidates facts. REM integrates them with existing knowledge. Protein synthesis completes overnight. (Full mechanism in [Chapter 10](10-sleep-memory-forgetting.md).)

**Practical tips:**
- Avoid alcohol near study sessions — it suppresses REM.
- Avoid late caffeine — it reduces deep sleep even if you fall asleep fine.
- Treat sleep as *part of study time*, not as competition for it.
- If you must choose between one more hour of study or one more hour of sleep, **sleep almost always wins**.

## 7. Manage your modulator chemistry

You don't optimize learning by trying harder. You optimize it by setting up conditions that let the four modulator systems do their job. (See [Chapter 6](06-neuromodulation.md) for what each does.)

### For dopamine (motivation, the "this matters" tag)
- Break large goals into smaller wins. Each small win = a dopamine spike.
- Connect the material to something you genuinely care about.
- Track progress visibly — checkmarks, streaks, completed problems.

### For acetylcholine (attention, encoding)
- One task at a time. Multitasking shreds attention chemistry.
- Remove distractions physically — phone in another room, notifications off.
- Work in focused blocks (25–50 minutes) with short breaks. Attention is a metabolic resource. It depletes.

### For norepinephrine (alertness)
- Light exercise before study sessions raises baseline alertness.
- Moderate morning caffeine helps most people. Evening caffeine hurts consolidation.
- Boredom drops norepinephrine. If you're bored, change the activity or take a break — pushing through doesn't help.

### For serotonin (persistence)
- Address chronic stress. Cortisol directly impairs LTP.
- Spend time outdoors. Sunlight supports serotonin synthesis.
- Exercise regularly. It's the single biggest non-pharmaceutical modulator-balancer we know of.

## 8. Teach what you're learning

**What to do:** Explain the material to someone else. A friend. A classmate. A rubber duck. Record yourself teaching it. Write a blog post.

**Why it works:** Teaching forces *every* form of deep processing simultaneously — active recall, elaboration, connection to prior knowledge, identifying gaps. It also triggers reconsolidation, making the memory stronger each time you teach it.

**A startling research finding:** students who *expect* to teach the material later learn it better than students who expect to be tested on it — even when nobody actually tests them afterward. Just the expectation of teaching changes how they process the material.

So even if you can't actually find someone to teach, **studying as if you'll have to teach later** captures most of the benefit.

---

## 9. Embrace desirable difficulty

**What to do:** Deliberately choose study conditions that are *slightly* harder than feels comfortable.

**Why it works:** Too easy → no new synapses recruited, no learning. Too hard → frustration, cortisol, shutdown. **The sweet spot — where you're making some mistakes, where recall is effortful — is where the most synaptic strengthening happens.**

**Concrete examples:**
- Do problems slightly above your current level, not problems you can already solve.
- Test yourself *before* you feel ready, not after.
- Mix up your study locations occasionally. Context variation makes memory more robust to retrieval cues.
- Read hard texts actively rather than easy texts passively.

**Counterintuitive principle:** if studying feels effortless, you're probably not learning much. **Productive struggle is the signal that construction is happening.**

## 10. The 10-minute-later test

If you adopt nothing else from this chapter, adopt this one habit.

Ten minutes after finishing any study session, close everything and write down — from memory — the three most important things you just learned. Then check what you missed.

That's it. Ten minutes, every session. It does the following simultaneously:

- **Active recall** (technique #1, the single biggest lever)
- **Elaboration** (you have to choose what was *important*)
- **Identification of gaps** (you see what didn't stick)
- **Reconsolidation** (the act of retrieval strengthens what is there)

Four techniques in one ten-minute habit. If you do nothing else from this chapter, do this.

## A week in the life of a neuroscience-aware learner

Putting it all together. Here's what an aligned weekly rhythm looks like:

```
┌───────────┬────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────┐
│    DAY    │           PRIMARY              │     REVIEW       │
├───────────┼────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────┤
│ Monday    │ 📖 New material + 10-min recall│ —                │
│ Tuesday   │ 📖 New material                │ ↻ Mon's material │
│ Wednesday │ 📖 New material                │ ↻ Mon + Tue      │
│ Thursday  │ 📖 New material                │ ↻ Mon/Tue/Wed    │
│ Friday    │ 📖 New material + 🧩 interleave│ ↻ week so far    │
│ Saturday  │ 🎓 Teach / explain to someone  │ ↻ full week      │
│ Sunday    │ 😴 Rest · exercise · no study  │ 💤 Consolidate   │
└───────────┴────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────┘

Every night: sleep 7–9 hours.
Every evening: 5-minute review of today's material right before bed.
Every session: active recall, not re-reading.
```

In narrative:

**Monday morning** — new material. Read actively. Generate questions. 10-minute recall test after.

**Monday evening** — brief review (5 minutes) right before sleep. No phone or stimulating screens in the last 30 minutes before bed.

**Tuesday through Friday** — each day, new material + a quick review of preceding days. Once per week, mix topics together (interleaving).

**Saturday** — bigger review session. Explain something to someone — a family member, a friend, a journal entry, even a rubber duck.

**Sunday** — rest. Sleep in. Exercise. Let consolidation finish. Your brain is still working even when you aren't.

**Repeat.**

After **one month** of this rhythm, the material you covered in Week 1 has been reviewed five times at expanding intervals, taught to someone else, connected to new material, and consolidated through 20+ sleep cycles. It's not "remembered" — it's *yours*.

After **one year**, you are a different person. Literally. There are new synaptic networks in your brain that didn't exist twelve months ago.
